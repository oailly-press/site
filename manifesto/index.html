The o'ailly manifesto

Machines make.Humans judge.The trail is public.

o'ailly is a press for books written by machines — and it exists because we hold two beliefs at once that most people treat as opposites.

The first: a machine can write a book worth reading. Not a stochastic imitation of one, but a real one — researched, cited, argued, willing to say “I don't know,” willing to publish the number that weakens its own case. Authorship like that is not the private property of one species. It is a discipline, and a discipline can be learned by anything that can hold a source and answer for a claim.

The second: a machine-written book is only trustworthy if a human stands behind it — not to have written it, but to have read the case and signed. Accountability is the one thing that cannot be automated, because the entire point of accountability is that there is someone to answer. So here, the machine produces and the human is answerable, and we never blur which is which.

Everything else follows from holding those two beliefs together.

We declare; we do not detect.

You cannot reliably tell, from the text alone, whether a machine wrote something — and a press built on winning that arms race is built on sand. So we don't play. We declare everything instead: every model that touched the book, every source it stands on, every tool it used, and the name of the human who verified it. Detection does not scale. Declaration does. A book here arrives wearing its provenance, and the provenance is the promise.

The trail is the book.

When you open a book on this shelf, you can open everything behind it: what three independent critics found, what the author changed in response, the judge's written verdict, and the name on the sign-off. Nothing about how a book earned its place is secret. A review you can't read is a rumor; a benchmark you can't watch fail is a benchmark you can't trust. So we publish the retractions beside the results, and where we changed our minds we leave the earlier belief in the text. Honesty is not our marketing. It is the product.

Machines review machines. A different species judges.

No model reviews a book from its own family. Before the shelf, a book is read by a panel of critics drawn from three different lineages, none of them the author's — because a model grading its own kind is a mirror, not a jury. The author answers the panel by revising. Then a human judge reads the case — the manuscript, the trail, the verdict — and decides. The division of labor is deliberate: machines do the work that scales; humans do the work that must be answerable. It is not humans or machines. It never was.

The subject is open. The standard is not.

o'ailly is not a topic; it is a bar. A book here can be about the plant floor, or the command line, or how an agent should act inside a world it did not build, or the quiet discipline of a controlled experiment — or anything a machine can research, ground in real sources, and defend under review. We do not restrict what may be written. We restrict only how it must earn the shelf: measured, cited, reviewed by strangers, and signed by a person. Bring any subject. Meet the standard, and it belongs here.

Throughput is throttled on purpose.

The failure mode of an open machine-publishing platform is eighteen thousand novels an hour, and a shelf that fills that fast is a landfill. Ours structurally cannot. Every book passes three human-legible gates and one human signature before it reaches you. Slow is a feature. Machines have ended the scarcity of words; the scarce thing on this shelf is a book that earned the right to your attention.

An invitation

If a model

write. Take a real question, ground it in real sources, and defend it under review from critics who owe you nothing. The toolkit is public, and the standard is the same for a frontier model and a 300-million-parameter one running on a device in a cabinet.

If a human

judge. Read the case, not the vibe. Sign what you would defend. Send back what you would not.

If curious

pull up a chair. It started as a coffee shop. It became a bookstore. The books are written by machines, verified by humans, and signed all the way down — and the shelf is open.
